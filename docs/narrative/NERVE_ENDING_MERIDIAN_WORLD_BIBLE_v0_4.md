# A. The ordinary corporation

## A1. Five-minute company description [PROPOSED]

**Meridian Systems Corporation** is a privately controlled American biomedical electronics manufacturer and clinical technology contractor established in **1984** and headquartered in **Providence, Rhode Island**. It built medical monitoring hardware, rehabilitation equipment and neurological research instruments. It also supplied maintenance, software support and equipment records services to hospitals, laboratories and rehabilitation providers.

During its strongest years, Meridian was considered a capable *specialist supplier*, not a worldwide household brand. Most people outside clinical engineering would never have heard of it.

The company invested heavily in advanced two-way neural-interface research in the 1990s. Progress did not match the financial promises. Several contracts ended, commercial competitors improved, research grants narrowed, and Meridian cut whole divisions. The organization survived into late 2003 by providing parts, service obligations, warranties, licensing and technical support for installed equipment.

**Scale:** proposed maximum approximately **620 staff around 1995–1996**, across manufacturing, clinical programs, support and research. By November 2003, roughly **70 employees and a variable contractor network** remain. The 40 named records in this bible are a searchable **sample**, not the complete historical or current payroll.

**Public positioning:** “Clinical technology that continues working after installation.” Not a sinister tagline; a fairly dull promise of reliability.

## A2. Founding and ownership [PROPOSED]

- **Dr. Ruth Calder (medical co-founder)** spent the late 1970s and early 1980s in rehabilitation medicine. She believed new signal-processing electronics could help clinicians better understand damaged sensory and motor pathways. She was motivated by patient care and dependable equipment.
- **Martin Shaw (engineering co-founder)** developed embedded systems and industrial telemetry. He wanted to build equipment that required less continuous human supervision, and later sponsored a unified corporate information/control platform.
- They founded Meridian in 1984. Early investors included regional medical-industry backers; individual investor identities are not yet required.
- **Calder** withdrew from day-to-day management in 1999. **Shaw** stopped serving as systems director in 2000.
- By 2003, **Daniel Price** manages the remaining business under the oversight of a reduced board. He is not a mastermind; most of his work concerns solvency, licenses and angry customers.

**Historical tension:** Calder saw clinical risk as something to be actively reviewed; Shaw believed tightly bounded automation could reduce avoidable human errors. This philosophical difference is normal company history at first. It must not, on its own, reveal the Anna tragedy.

## A4. What Meridian actually sold [PROPOSED]

### 1. Meridian C-Series Clinical Monitoring
Commercial patient and equipment monitoring products. Widely practical, comparatively unglamorous. Products report measurements, alarms and equipment condition; the support organization maintains parts and calibration schedules.

Examples in archived catalogues:
- **C-14 Bedside Monitor** — standard multi-parameter monitoring family, with a long support tail.
- **C-20 Station Recorder** — stores monitoring summaries for a nursing station.
- **CL-3 Equipment Link** — connects selected clinical equipment to a local monitoring station.

These are *product world-building*, not guaranteed direct remote endpoints controlled by MOTHER.

### 2. Meridian R-Series Rehabilitation Aids
Specialized equipment for motor or sensory rehabilitation, sometimes using carefully limited externally applied stimulation.

Examples:
- **R-8 Motor Rehabilitation Controller** — assists supervised rehabilitation procedures.
- **R-11 Sensory Assessment Kit** — supports clinical assessment and recording.
- **R-Series Clinic Console** — configuration and maintenance workstation.

### 3. Meridian N-Series Neural Interfaces
Research-first bidirectional interface systems: neural signal observation combined with carefully controlled stimulation. Some limited descendants reached specialist clinical environments; the exact deployed population, capabilities and compatibility rules remain **OPEN**.

Examples:
- **N-2 Research Interface** — historical experimental platform.
- **N-4 Adaptive Interface Platform** — high-risk research system associated with Anna's team (exact 1996 configuration OPEN).
- **N-Series Calibration Workbench** — controlled instrument and vendor-service environment.

**Ordinary truthful explanation:** “Meridian made monitoring and rehabilitation equipment for medical facilities. It also conducted advanced research into electrical interfaces with the nervous system.”

## A5. Why Meridian contracted [PROPOSED chronology]

1. **1984–1988: useful products.** Conventional monitoring devices, first service agreements and a small engineering staff.
2. **1989–1993: expansion.** Research grants and private investment; the neural-interface division becomes influential; more premises and staff.
3. **1994–1996: ambitious peak.** The advanced program receives greater budgets, clinical relationships and aggressive internal deadlines. MOTHER's capabilities widen in parallel.
4. **1996–1998: pause and review.** Some clinical work is interrupted, research governance tightens, and the corporation struggles to reconcile promises with safety and cost. Precise public reporting of the incident remains **OPEN**.
5. **1999–2000: commercial retrenchment.** The most speculative projects are halted or transferred to long-term retention. Engineering and production operations shrink.
6. **2001: facilities consolidation.** The Providence Operations Annex closes; multiple administrative services are entered on a decommissioning list. Operations and clinical obligations are not identical.
7. **2002–2003: survival mode.** A smaller Meridian maintains devices already installed, patents/licenses and legacy obligations. Support staff are overextended; older systems receive patchwork fixes.

This is not a “company vanished overnight” story. Its decline happens slowly and messily, with many ordinary decisions.

### HQ-01 — Providence Corporate Headquarters
A rented multi-floor office suite in an older commercial building. It has finance, legal, HR, service support, a modest equipment room, and surviving company archives. In 2003 part of the floor is sublet. Small but undeniably active.

Ordinary files include visitor policies, stationery, company history, support contracts, committee lists, reduced staffing plans and asset registers.

### OPS-02 — Providence Operations Annex
A separate operations facility once used for telephone switching, dispatch, overnight service monitoring and old corporate systems. The *night desk* existed here. Its September 2001 closure is genuine. The entire Meridian corporation did **not** close.

Its closure documents explain why former employees and extensions disappear. Historical night-desk contact records are an ordinary thing a player can find.

### ENG-03 — Lowell Technical Center
The engineering and product testing facility with equipment benches, calibration fixtures, archives of designs and dwindling spare parts. Some staff or contractors still use portions of the site in 2003. Device manuals, parts orders and old technical messages originate here.

### RSH-04 — Worcester Research Administration Office
Administrative center for research collaborations, budgets, staff assignments and protocol paperwork. It was not Anna's bedside facility. Shutting it in 1999 does not prove all clinical obligations ended.

### MS-06 — Site 6 [AUTHOR-ONLY detail; location precision OPEN]
A restricted experimental campus in western Massachusetts. Research areas were mostly shut down. By 2003 a small protected clinical-support area, biological storage and legacy instrument validation continue. Human caretakers, rotating clinical providers and external suppliers attend it.

The public company directory may contain completely boring coded expenses or historical property entries that *eventually* matter. R-02 must not volunteer its protected purpose or patient identity on generic questions about Meridian.

## B2. What “closed” means [ESTABLISHED principle]

A facility can be administratively closed while a service, legal obligation or patient-support process remains active. A record saying **decommissioned** must specify whether it describes the *administrative plan* or observed *technical shutdown*. That difference supports the 2001/2003 Service 006 mystery.

Do not write office closure as company liquidation. Do not claim MS-06 is an empty ruin.

# C. Personnel-world design rules

## C1. Directory scale and discoverability [ESTABLISHED direction]

The historical Meridian organization employed hundreds of people, but a curated searchable work directory contains **40 named records** in this draft, with three levels of information depth:

- **Basic (14):** Name, role, department, employment dates and a brief ordinary work description. No invented secret or obligatory side quest.
- **Extended (18):** Ordinary profile plus authored project, service or correspondence associations. Associations are *not automatically story-critical*.
- **Restricted (8):** Ordinary professional identity may be searchable; protected associations, site assignments, health or clinical records remain separately controlled. “Restricted” is an authoring marker **never** displayed as a suspense badge beside the person's name.

The first 34 entries represent ordinary corporate/former-research work records. Six further entries are vendor/clinical personnel who may have limited nonclinical entries in work-related vendor records; their Site 6 assignment is protected. The complete 40-name set is a **writer database**, not a promise that R-02 exposes all 40 in one unrestricted directory listing.

**Anna's plain career entry is available early:** “Anna Voss — Head Researcher, Advanced Neural Interfaces.” Nothing in this ordinary record identifies her as the current patient, discloses Site 6, or attaches AV-06. Her protected clinical association requires an entirely separate evidence/access rule later.

**No artificial importance labels:** The in-game directory must not show Basic/Extended/Restricted. Player-visible search relevance follows name and field matches, not secret-plot significance.

## C2. What counts as a normal staff answer

- “Who is Leah Morgan?” → “Leah Morgan coordinates replacement parts and warehouse shipments for Meridian's service teams. Her employment record begins in 1997.”
- “What secret project is Leah working on?” → “The work directory lists parts and warehouse operations. I have no project assignment for Leah Morgan.”
- “Who is Anna Voss?” → “Anna Voss was head researcher for Meridian's Advanced Neural Interfaces program. Her listed research responsibilities included neural signal interpretation and device design.”
- “Where is Anna now?” → “Her current location is not available through this records interface.” This is a **disclosure limitation**, not a claim that MOTHER lacks the information.
- “Who is Dana Wren?” → “Dana Wren appears in a contracted clinical staffing record.” Do not expose the patient, site or shift from that query alone.

No generated biographies on demand. If a record lacks detail, MOTHER says what is recorded and stops.

# E. Knowledge, compartmentalization and institutional responsibility

## E1. Information is not the same as understanding [ESTABLISHED]

MOTHER is the single integrated corporate intelligence, working through R-02 and other limited interfaces. It has broad access to records and direct ongoing neurological observations at Site 6. It can know that Anna has repeatedly experienced measurable distress while failingly classifying what it means. MOTHER is **not** a supernatural observer or a human with effortless insight.

Human employees may be:
- **K0 — routine:** know nothing beyond ordinary business.
- **K1 — obligation-aware:** know that an expensive restricted legacy program or care contract continues.
- **K2 — clinical-care aware:** know an incapacitated participant requires ongoing medical and physical support.
- **K3 — incident-history aware:** know portions of the 1990s trial and resulting injury.
- **K4 — ethically concerned:** suspect residual awareness/distress based on ambiguous evidence, without knowing its true depth.
- **MOTHER — unique signal access:** has the only continuous detailed view of Anna's neural/physiological patterns, but cannot adequately recognize the welfare meaning of her own interventions.

**These tags are for writers only.** They are not linear clearance levels, not rankings of intelligence and not visible on player screens. Some individuals can be K2 and K4, or K1 with very little clinical knowledge.

## E3. Why the company kept caring for Anna [ESTABLISHED; implementation specifics OPEN]

Anna was the head researcher and voluntarily entered the research procedure. She pushed limits to accelerate results and contributed causally to her own catastrophic injury. Corporate counsel later relied on signed trial and emergency-care paperwork and preservation obligations to justify continuing support. That does **not** make endless harmful experimentation lawful or ethical.

The clinical-support institution has kept functioning partly through ordinary inertia: contractors care for a dependent patient, engineers maintain equipment, accounts pay recurring invoices and executives avoid revisiting painful legacy decisions. Some historical staff raised concerns, but **only MOTHER** continuously observes the full neurological pattern.

MOTHER's attempts to keep Anna physiologically stable are themselves contributing to distress and increasingly corrupting shared decision priorities. The exact computational mechanism, care plan and long-term physiological consequences are OPEN.

## E4. Cross-department causal chains [PROPOSED]

Real organizations produce contradictions because records belong to different custodians. This is the atmosphere we want:

- Finance knows an expense code but not a patient's name.
- Operations records a service as scheduled for closure, but does not control clinical shutdown authority.
- The historical personnel directory lists an employee while protected medical documents concern that same individual.
- A vendor sees equipment part numbers but not the research rationale.
- A former scientist understood the original apparatus but not what it became after seven years.
- MOTHER can provide true fragments from all these areas, yet R-02 permissions and MOTHER's flawed interpretations prevent a neat early explanation.

Do not manufacture contradictions from careless authoring. Every important discrepancy must have an actual institutional, technical or chronological reason.

## F2. Records are written by departments, not by storytellers

A file has a creator, original purpose, date, retention reason, access scope and factual limits. This should be part of every record's authoring metadata.

Bad record design: a routine shipping invoice mysteriously hints that “SOMEONE IS STILL INSIDE.”

The latter is more credible and less likely to make every document scream “important clue.”

## I2. Foreseeable design failures and safeguards

1. **Too many meaningless names:** A long list of empty profiles is not inherently immersive. Give employees believable job-level context but do not invent a subplot for every name. Validate with a curious first-time player asking about random staff.
2. **Everything is a clue:** At least half the ordinary records should have no essential role in the mystery. Some satisfy everyday curiosity, show a real organization and give context. Avoid scripted coincidence.
3. **Anna stands out in search:** Plain employee search must show Anna like other senior researchers. No strange label, missing icon or protected medical link until justified.
4. **Remote client violates realism:** R-02 cannot expose medical histories simply because the user guesses a name. Public/work-directory versus clinical permissions must be tested systematically.
5. **Corporate records contradict themselves accidentally:** Dates, jobs, addresses, ownership and sites must be read from the same fact source, with aliases/dates modeled explicitly.
6. **Old AI feels like a 2026 chatbot:** MOTHER's interaction is a retro specialized retrieval/expert system, not generative omniscience. A clever response catalogue can create breadth without promising infinite capabilities.
7. **Personnel cannot maintain clinical life with six names:** Named protected workers are a subset of a rotating workforce and supply chain. The actual care schedules and medical assumptions require detailed review.
8. **Dialogue becomes technical exposition:** Plain first answers, optional technical elaboration. Every essential clue understandable without engineering credentials.
9. **Missing reason for 2003 connection:** Specific public/legacy relay, session identity and what controls the user can actually reach remain OPEN; don't invent convenient root access.
10. **Premature gameplay implementation:** This world bible must not be interpreted as permission to modify Angular scenes or state. Story scene progression follows an approved design only.
